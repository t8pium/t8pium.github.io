Do Fair Value Gaps predict price?

A falsification-style study of MNQ futures from 2020–2026. Instead of asking whether FVGs “fill,” the project asks whether the FVG label adds measurable information beyond distance, volatility, trend, session, and ordinary price revisits.

Conclusion: FVGs are not statistically empty, but their predictive information is weak, short-lived, and context-dependent. The strongest repeatable evidence is a small lower-timeframe attraction bias in the first few bars and a small retest-reaction effect. The data does not support a persistent magnetic force or a standalone edge.

Research question

Do mechanically defined Fair Value Gaps contain incremental information about where MNQ price travels next, or can their apparent behavior be explained by ordinary market structure?

The study deliberately separates predictability from profitability. A pattern can shift a probability by a few percentage points and still be too weak to trade after costs, stop geometry, adverse excursion, and model-selection error.

Mechanical definition

For completed candles A = t−2, B = t−1, C = t:

  • Bullish FVG: Low[C] > High[A]. Zone = High[A] → Low[C].
  • Bearish FVG: High[C] < Low[A]. Zone = High[C] → Low[A].
  • The FVG exists only after candle C has closed. No experiment may act as though the gap existed earlier.

The active research series selected the highest-volume listed MNQ contract for each CME trading date and excluded roll neighborhoods from event/control formation to avoid synthetic roll gaps.

What the fill rate hides

The raw result sounds dramatic: about 90.9% of 1m FVGs were touched within 60 minutes, about 98.2% within roughly a trading day, and about 99.88% were eventually revisited somewhere later in the dataset.

But the matched controls are the important part. At roughly three trading days, FVG touch probability was about 98.95% while comparable ordinary zones were about 98.97%. The long-horizon “magnet” disappears.

Core lesson: a nearby price level can have an enormous eventual revisit rate without containing unique information. The relevant statistic is the incremental difference versus a baseline, not the raw fill percentage.

Where the effect is strongest

The cleanest attraction pattern appeared immediately after lower-timeframe formation. In the expanded matched study, 1m FVGs were reached +3.43 percentage points more often than controls by three bars and +2.70 pp more often by five bars. On 5m the five-bar effect was +2.16 pp.

The effect then decayed. In the deeper 1m study, the difference fell from +3.03 pp at 5 minutes to +0.58 pp at 60 minutes, approximately zero by one trading day, and slightly negative by roughly three trading days.

Higher-timeframe attraction did not scale upward. The 4H matched attraction test was negative at every tested early horizon, including −4.79 pp after one 4H bar.

Reaction, not gravity

A different experiment waited until price returned to the FVG and then compared its reaction with a matched ordinary zone. Here the direction was positive across every tested timeframe: roughly +2.32 pp on 1m, +1.22 pp on 5m, +1.41 pp on 15m, +0.91 pp on 1H, and +1.55 pp on 4H.

That is small, but it suggests the more stable property may be how price behaves at an FVG, not a persistent force pulling price toward it.

CE / midpoint

The midpoint tests were deliberately separated from the general retest tests. Starting from the midpoint and racing the near edge against the far edge produced FVG-vs-control advantages of +2.45 pp on 5m, +2.64 pp on 15m, and +4.39 pp on 4H, while 1m and 1H were weak.

A separate execution study found that an exact 50% body close was not strong. The cleaner ≥1H sample had only 51 exact-midpoint closes and an unambiguous win rate of roughly 36.4%.

The exploratory anomaly was on 4H: body closes 45–50% deep into the gap produced 33 wins / 16 losses, 67.35% WR and +0.286R/trade. Because that cell was discovered after scanning many timeframes and bands, it is treated as a new hypothesis—not evidence of a confirmed edge.

Controlled model

A 60-minute logistic model used FVG status alongside ATR-normalized distance, width, volatility, trend, time of day, and direction. The FVG indicator corresponded to an odds ratio of about 1.079.

That sounds larger than the raw probability effect because the baseline touch probability is already high. In probability terms, the advantage remained only around half to one percentage point at 60 minutes.

Distance was substantially more important. Zones within 0.25 ATR were touched around 97.6% of the time whether or not they were FVGs. The FVG label matters less than geometry when the target is already extremely close.

Interpretation

The most conservative explanation is that a three-candle FVG is a marker of a temporary displacement state. The process that created the gap may leave short-lived structure in subsequent retracement and reaction probabilities. That does not mean the rectangle itself causally pulls price.

This interpretation is compatible with broader market-microstructure research showing that order-flow and queue imbalance can predict very short-term movement, and with empirical support/resistance research showing local reaction effects that decay with age. Those adjacent literatures make the phenomenon plausible; they do not validate FVG theory by themselves.

Limitations

  • One primary market: MNQ. Cross-market replication is still required.
  • Base resolution is 1-minute OHLCV, so low-timeframe TP/SL ordering can be ambiguous.
  • Matched synthetic controls reduce obvious confounding but cannot control every hidden market structure.
  • Many events from the same day/move are dependent; day-level resampling was used where practical.
  • Multiple timeframe/band searches create data-mining risk. Strong subgroup discoveries must be independently replicated.
  • Statistical effects are not automatically economically tradeable after spread, commissions, slippage, and adverse excursion.

Final answer

Do FVGs have predictable strength? Yes, slightly. In this MNQ sample the FVG label contains weak but measurable information, mostly in the first few bars after formation and around certain retests. That information decays, varies by regime and timeframe, and is far too conditional to support “FVGs are magnets” or “FVG alone is an edge.”
